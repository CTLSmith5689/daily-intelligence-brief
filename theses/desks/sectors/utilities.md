# Utilities

Electric, gas and water utilities, multi-utilities, independent power producers and renewable generators. Owned by the Energy, materials and utilities desk.

## The questions that decide a stock

1. How fast is the rate base growing? For a regulated utility, profit is roughly the allowed return times the capital the regulator lets it earn on, so growth in that capital is growth in earnings.
2. What return does the regulator allow, and does the company earn it? The allowed return on equity sets the ceiling; delays between spending and new rates eat into it.
3. How is the capital spending paid for? Most utilities spend more than they generate, so the gap is filled with debt or new shares, and new shares dilute holders.
4. How good is the relationship with the regulator? Rate cases, disallowed costs and wildfire or storm liabilities decide years.
5. For power producers and renewables: how much output is sold under long contracts, and how much at market prices?

## How to value it

- **Regulated utilities.** P/E against the growth in rate base and earnings per share, plus the dividend yield. Price to book is a useful cross-check, because the regulator sets the return on book equity.
- **Power producers.** EV/EBITDA, with the contracted share of output and the power price as the drivers.
- **Water utilities.** P/E, usually higher than electric utilities because rate base growth is steadier.

## Traps

- Judging a regulated utility on free cash flow. It is usually negative by design, because the company spends ahead of the rates that pay for the spending.
- Reading a high dividend yield as safe without checking how much new debt and equity the capital plan needs.
- Treating a power producer's peak-price year as normal.

## Where our data misleads

Checked against the panel of 2026-09-23, on the 63 companies in this sector that pass the screen's size and revenue gate.

- 42 of the 63 had negative free cash flow over the past year, and the median free cash flow yield is negative 4.5%. A free cash flow screen ranks nearly the whole sector as poor.
- Median net debt is 5.5 times EBITDA. That is normal for a regulated utility and would be alarming elsewhere, so compare only within the sector.
- The panel has no field for rate base, allowed return on equity or rate case dates. Those come only from the filing text.
