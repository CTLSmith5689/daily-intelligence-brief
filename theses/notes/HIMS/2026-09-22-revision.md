## WHAT THE COMPANY DOES

Hims & Hers sells treatments for a short list of conditions: hair loss, sexual health, weight loss, skin, mental health and hormone health, plus laboratory testing. A customer answers questions on its website or app, is connected to a licensed provider who can write a prescription, and the medicine arrives by post. Most prescriptions are filled by pharmacies the company owns. Most customers pay by subscription and are billed each time a shipment goes out.

It had 2.9 million subscribers at the end of June 2026, each paying $92 a month on average. It sells in the United States, Canada, the United Kingdom and parts of Europe, and since June 2026 in Australia and Japan. It owns two pharmacies in the United States and two in the United Kingdom, a facility that mixes sterile medicines, a plant that makes peptides and a testing laboratory. It also sells non-prescription products such as skincare and supplements in shops.

## HOW IT MAKES MONEY

Three things decide the profit. They are how many subscribers there are, how much each pays a month, and what is left of each $1 of sales after the cost of the product.

In the three months to June 2026 the company sold $753 million, up 38 percent from a year earlier. Subscribers rose 19 percent and the average monthly payment rose to $92 from $76. Of each $1 of sales, 64 cents was left after the cost of the product, against 76 cents a year earlier. Management gives four reasons. Weight loss treatments now ship more often and cost more to send. The business outside the United States has grown. There was a one-off write-down of stock, and the companies it bought keep less of each sale.

The largest cost after the product is finding customers. That cost $230 million in the quarter against $753 million of sales, up from $188 million a year earlier.

Where the sales grew matters. Sales in the United States rose 16 percent in the quarter and 3 percent over the six months. Sales elsewhere went from $7.5 million to $131 million in the quarter, almost all of it from companies it bought.

## THE LAST TEN YEARS

I have seven years of filed figures, 2019 to 2025. Sales rose every year, from $82.6 million to $2,348 million. Profit is another matter. The company lost money in five of those seven years. It made $126 million in 2024 and $128 million in 2025, its only profitable years, which came to $0.53 and $0.51 for each share. Then it lost $178 million in the six months to June 2026.

So the typical year in this company's record is a loss. Its two profitable years each earned about $0.50 for a share that now costs $30.43.

Profit moves this far because two large costs move with the sales. The cost of the product took 24 cents of each $1 of sales in the June 2025 quarter and 36 cents a year later. Finding customers took another 31 cents. Small changes in either turn a profit into a loss.

## WHAT MANAGEMENT DOES WITH THE CASH

The business does not yet fund itself. In the six months to June 2026 its operations brought in $53 million. It spent $69 million on equipment, websites and software over the same months, which is $15 million more than it took in. It pays no dividend and buys back no shares.

The cash has gone into buying other companies. It paid $153 million for YourBio in January 2026. It agreed to pay $968 million for Eucalyptus in June 2026. That was $225 million in cash at the start and $684 million in six quarterly payments over the following 18 months. Up to $97 million more is due if targets are met. It also paid $46 million of earlier earn-out money for Zava.

To pay for that it raised $403 million by issuing notes in May 2026 that pay no interest and fall due in 2032. In July 2026 it agreed to sell up to $400 million of money owed by customers to a bank, at a discount. It held $841 million of cash and short-term investments at the end of June. What it owes within a year now exceeds what it holds within a year by $99 million. Six months earlier there was a surplus of $363 million. It may settle most of the Eucalyptus payments in new shares instead, up to 19.9 percent of its ordinary shares.

## WHAT HAS TO BE TRUE FOR THE PRICE TO MAKE SENSE

The shares closed at $30.43, which is 51 percent below their highest price of the past year. On the share count in the 2025 accounts, all the shares together are worth about $7.9 billion.

That is 61 times the $128 million the company earned in 2025, its best year. The company forecasts sales of $3.1 billion to $3.3 billion for 2026, so the price is about 2.5 times the sales for each share that it expects this year.

The price makes sense only if the profit of 2025 comes back and then grows, on sales half as large again. It also needs the share of each $1 kept after product costs to stop falling.

## WHERE I DISAGREE

The note on Hims & Hers written on 12 September 2026 made this claim:

> Hims & Hers Health sells health care services by subscription, and part of its sales come from pharmacy-made copies of the newer weight-loss drugs. Its fast sales growth is the figure I trust least, because I think the sales that are speeding up are the ones regulators are cutting off. I would stay away from the stock.

I am REPLACING that claim. The conclusion stands and the reason does not. That note was written without any of the company's own filings, and it expected regulators to stop the sales. The filings show the company did that itself. In the first quarter of 2026 it announced a change to its United States weight loss offering, moving to branded medicines and offering pharmacy-made copies only on a limited scale.

Here is what I believe now. The growth on the front of the results announcement came from buying other companies, and those sales keep less of each $1 after product costs. Sales in the United States, which are most of the company, grew 3 percent over the six months. Total sales grew 20 percent, and companies bought this year supplied the difference. Of each $1 of sales, 12 cents less is left after product costs than a year earlier. Management says it expects that figure to stay below last year's in the near term, because of the weight loss change and the companies it bought.

The figures that show what the growth cost sit below the ones that show the growth. The announcement of 10 August 2026 opens with sales up 38 percent and a raised forecast for the year. The 3 percent figure for the United States is in a table further down it. The $684 million still owed for Eucalyptus is in the quarterly report filed the same day, and not in the announcement at all.

I would stay away rather than bet against these shares. Their price has ranged from $14.52 to $62.76 over the past year, and a single piece of news could lift them by a third without my view being wrong.

## WHAT WOULD PROVE ME WRONG

One result would prove me wrong. Hims & Hers may keep 70 cents or more of each $1 of sales, after the cost of its products, in the three months to 30 September 2026. Then the fall in that figure was a one-off, and the reason for my view goes with it.

The strongest argument against me is that this is a subscription business still adding customers. Subscribers rose 19 percent over the year and the average monthly payment rose $16. The company forecasts sales of $3.1 billion to $3.3 billion for the year, which needs about $1.8 billion in the second half against $1.4 billion in the first. It also forecasts $275 million to $325 million of a profit figure of its own. That figure leaves out interest, tax, wear and tear, share-based pay, legal charges and the cost of buying companies. It holds $841 million, and it may pay most of what it owes for Eucalyptus in shares.

My view survives that argument. Paying in shares would leave existing holders with up to 19.9 percent less of the company. The forecast the company highlights leaves out the costs that turned its half-year into a $178 million loss, including a $63 million legal charge. And none of those figures answers the test above, which is about what is left of each $1 of sales.
