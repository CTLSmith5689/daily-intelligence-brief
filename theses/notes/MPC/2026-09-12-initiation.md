Marathon Petroleum runs oil refineries. It sells the fuel to other companies in bulk, to independent
Marathon and ARCO fuel stations, and abroad. Its business has three
parts: refining and selling fuel, pipelines and storage run through MPLX, and renewable diesel.

## WHAT HAS TO BE TRUE FOR THE PRICE TO MAKE SENSE

Marathon's shares closed at $392.42. That is near their highest price of the past year, and more
than double their price a year earlier. The shares cost about 14 times Marathon's profit per share
over the past year. For every $100 of stock, Marathon made about $12 of spare cash last year, more
than most refiners I compared it with.

These numbers pull two ways. A price that has more than doubled only makes sense if today's high
refining profits last a good while. A price of about 14 times profit only makes sense if those
profits fall back in the end, because profits that stayed this high would be worth more. I think a
price resting on both is normal for a refiner after strong years, so I don't read it as wrong.

## WHERE I DISAGREE

I don't disagree, and I will not make up a view to fill this section.

I think Marathon's strong growth mostly reflects the gap between what fuel sells for and what crude
oil costs, and Marathon sets neither price. I cannot tell that apart from good management without
figures on that gap. Marathon's scores against other refiners for growth, value, price trend and
quality also all agree, so they give me no obvious question to start from.

The same week, I chose to own CF Industries, a fertiliser maker whose profits also swing with prices
it does not set. CF has almost no debt, which gave me a reason to own it without guessing where
prices go. I have no such reason for Marathon.

Pipeline and storage fees are steadier than refining profits. So a company earning mostly from those
fees deserves a much higher price for the same profit than one earning mostly from refining. I
cannot see how Marathon's profit splits between refining, pipelines and renewable diesel, so I cannot
tell which kind of company I am looking at.

## WHAT WOULD SETTLE IT

I claim no gap between price and value, so nothing needs to close. Marathon's report for July to
September 2026 should answer the question. It will show the profit of each part and how many barrels
a day its refineries handled. I began gathering results announcements on 12 September 2026 and have no
older ones, so this is the first quarter I can check at all.

## WHAT THE SHARES COULD BE WORTH

I am not giving a bad, middle and good case. Each would rest on the future gap between fuel and
crude oil prices, which swings and which I cannot see. Three prices built on that would be guesses
dressed as analysis.

My target price is $392, essentially the latest close of $392.42, because I claim no advantage over
today's price. This note will not count in my record of predictions. A note with no view is
research, and counting it would water down my record with a call I never made.

## WHAT WOULD PROVE ME WRONG

I claim nothing, so nothing can prove me wrong. Either of two results would give me a view:

- Marathon's results for July to September 2026 show its pipeline and storage business earning more
  than 40% of the combined profit of its business parts.
- Its refineries handle less than 2.6 million barrels a day, in two quarters in a row.

I set both lines myself, and I cannot yet see Marathon's figures for either. The first would mean
more of Marathon's profit comes from steady fees. I think a company like that would be worth more
than about 14 times its profit. The second would mean its refining business is shrinking, since two
low quarters in a row is more than the usual ups and downs.

The strongest argument against my stance is that the shares already look cheap: about 14 times
profit, and about $12 of spare cash for every $100 of stock. My stance survives it, because I cannot
see how much of that profit is from refining, which could fall back.
