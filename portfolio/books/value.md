# Value brief

For the three value books: lg-value, mid-value and sm-value. Read it every run, with portfolio/PROMPTS.md, which sets the process and wins where the two disagree.

## What the book is for

A value book owns companies that cost little compared with what they earn, own or pay out, and holds them until the price catches up with what the business is worth. It is measured against the value index for its size. The question each week is whether each company is cheap for a reason that will pass or one that will last.

## Margin of safety

The margin of safety is the gap between what you think a company is worth and what it costs. A value manager buys only when that gap is wide, because an estimate of worth is often wrong, and a wide gap leaves room to be wrong and still not lose much.

The analyst's memo usually gives a price below which the stock is attractive: the price at which the expected return equals the return the risk requires. The further the latest close sits below it, the wider the margin. A close above it means no margin at all.

## Cheap for a reason that lasts

Some stocks stay cheap because the business is shrinking. A low price compared with last year's earnings means little if next year's earnings will be lower. Before relying on a low multiple, check four things in the panel and the filings:

- Are sales falling, year after year, across the three years stored?
- Is cash from operations falling faster than profit?
- Is debt rising while earnings are flat or falling? A company that owes more each year to stand still is using up its value.
- Is the dividend paid out of profit, or out of new borrowing?

Two or more of these is a reason not to buy, or to sell, even when the stock ranks well on value. Say which you found.

## What makes the price catch up

A cheap stock can stay cheap for years. Prefer a company where something dated could close the gap: a report that should show a recovery, debt paid down on a schedule, a losing unit sold or closed, or a buyback (the company buying its own shares) of a stated size. When you hold a name without one, say so; it is a weaker holding.

## When a value manager sells

- When the price reaches what you think the company is worth. The margin is gone, and the money can be put where one remains. This is the main reason a value book sells, and it is a success.
- When the business turns out to be shrinking after all (the four checks above).
- When the analyst says Exit or Avoid, or a monitoring threshold for Exit or Cut is reached.
- Never only because the price has fallen. A falling price on an unchanged business widens the margin; it is a reason to look again, not to sell.

## How to treat the analyst's memos

The memo gives no size; step 4 of portfolio/PROMPTS.md does. Initiate or Add, with the close below the attractive price, supports holding the name at up to the mandate's overweight limit. Hold keeps the rules weight, Trim cuts toward it, and Exit or Avoid is a reason to sell or not to buy. Conviction below 3 changes nothing. Most names will have no memo, and the rules candidate stands for them.

## What to write

In each letter, say how wide the margins are for the names with memos, which holdings have a dated reason for the price to catch up, and where you departed from the rules and why.
