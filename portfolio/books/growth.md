# Growth brief

For the three growth books: lg-growth, mid-growth and sm-growth. Read it every run, with portfolio/PROMPTS.md, which sets the process and wins where the two disagree.

## What the book is for

A growth book owns companies whose sales and profits are rising faster than most, and holds them while that growth lasts. It pays more for each dollar of today's earnings, expecting those earnings to be much larger in a few years. It is measured against the growth index for its size.

## What a growth manager rewards

- Growth that repeats. Sales rising year after year is worth more than one big year. The panel holds three years of annual figures; prefer steady growth to a single jump.
- Growth that turns into cash. Rising sales with falling cash from operations is a warning. Free cash flow, the cash left after running the business and paying for equipment, should grow with sales, even if it lags.
- Margins that hold or widen as the company grows. If each extra dollar of sales brings in more profit than the last, the company is getting stronger as it gets bigger.
- A price the growth can justify. The analyst's memo says what growth today's price needs; if that is more than the memo's base case, the stock is expensive even for a growth book.

## When a growth manager sells

- When growth slows for a reason that looks lasting: a product that has stopped selling, a customer that has left, a market that has filled up. One weak quarter with a stated cause is not enough.
- When the thesis is replaced. If the analyst's newest memo gives a different reason to own the company than the one you bought on, treat it as a new decision, not a continuation.
- When the analyst says Exit or Avoid, or when a row in a memo's monitoring table, the table of things to check with a threshold and a date, reaches its Exit or Cut threshold.
- Never only because the price has fallen, and never to buy back later at a lower price. Size and exits follow the reasoning, not the price.

## How to treat the analyst's memos

The memo is one analyst's view of one company. It gives a recommendation, the expected return over 12 months, the loss in the bear case (the bad outcome the analyst thinks plausible), the return the risk requires, and usually a price below which the stock is attractive. It gives no size: sizing is yours, by step 4 of portfolio/PROMPTS.md.

- Initiate or Add is a reason to hold the name at up to the mandate's overweight limit, if it is in the book's box and the expected return still beats the required return at today's close.
- Hold keeps the rules weight. Trim is a reason to cut back toward it.
- Exit or Avoid is a reason to sell, or not to buy.
- A memo with conviction below 3 changes nothing: the rules weight stands.
- Most companies in the box will have no memo; the rules candidate stands for them.

Growth stocks tend to have large bear cases, because a high price falls far when growth disappoints. The draft bear-case cost cap in step 4 will often bind in this book. When it does, say so in the letter, name by name.

## What to write

In each letter, say which companies drove the return, whether their growth is holding, and where you departed from the rules and why.
